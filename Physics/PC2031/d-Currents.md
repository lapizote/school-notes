### Definition
Current in a wire is defined as the charge per time passing a given point.
Its unit is Ampere (A) = Coulomb/sec, and can also be expressed as
$$
\vec{I} = \lambda \vec{v}
$$
where $\lambda$ is the line charge density and $\vec{v}$ is the velocity of movement of charges.

We define positive charge moving at $v$, and negative charge moving at $-v$ (only a convention).

In many cases, only the magnitude is required, because direction of current depends on wire shape.

A current-carrying wire has a magnetic force, defined as
$$
\vec{F}_\text{mag} = \int I(d\vec{l} \times \vec{B}) = \int(\vec{v}\times \vec{B})\,dq = \int(\vec{v} \times \vec{B})\lambda \,dl = \int (\vec{I} \times \vec{B})\,dl = \int I(d\vec{l} \times \vec{B})
$$
### Surface and Volume Distributions of Current
##### Volume
Volume current density can be defined as
$$
\vec{J} = \rho \vec{v}
$$
where the unit of $\vec{J}$ is $A / m^2$ (current per unit area).

Note that we can express the infinitesimal current as
$$
\begin{align}
d\vec{I} &= \rho \vec{v} \,da_\perp \\
dI &= \vec{J} \cdot d\vec{a} = \rho \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{a}
\end{align}
$$
so then we can define current per unit area as
$$
\vec{J} = \frac{d\vec{I}}{da_\perp}
$$
The resulting magnetic force would then be
$$
\vec{F}_\text{mag} = \int(\vec{v}\times \vec{B})\,dq = \int(\vec{v}\times \vec{B})\rho\,d\tau = \int(\vec{J}\times \vec{B})\,d\tau
$$
Through Ohm's Law:
$$
\vec{J}=\sigma_c\vec{E}
$$
###### Properties
A current crossing a surface $\mathcal{S}$ of the volume can be expressed as
$$
I = \int_S J\, da_\perp = \int_S \vec{J}\cdot d\vec{a}
$$
If we have that surface enclose the entire volume $\mathcal{V}$:
$$
\int_\mathcal{V} (\nabla \cdot \vec{J})\,d\tau
$$
(This is essentially the Divergence Theorem)

It must equate to the change of net charge in the volume, such that
$$
\int_V (\nabla \cdot \vec{J})\,d\tau = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_V \rho\,d\tau = -\int_V\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} \right)\,d\tau
$$
or
$$
\nabla \cdot \vec{J} = -\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
$$
wherein for magnetostatics, it is equal to 0.
##### Surface
Surface current density is defined as
$$
\vec{K} = \sigma \vec{v}
$$
where $\vec{K}$ is $A / m$ (current per unit width).

We can express infinitesimal current as
$$
d\vec{I} = \sigma \vec{v} \,dl_\perp
$$
such that
$$
\vec{K} = \frac{d\vec{I}}{dl_\perp}
$$
The resulting magnetic force would be
$$
\vec{F}_\text{mag} = \int(\vec{v}\times \vec{B})\,dq = \int(\vec{v} \times \vec{B})\sigma\,da = \int(\vec{K}\times \vec{B})\,da
$$
Note that $\vec{B}$ experiences discontinuity across a current-carrying surface. For the above equation's purposes, it can be taken as the average of the field above and below the surface.

Also note that the current produces its own magnetic field, so in real cases the total field can be varying as the two fields interact with each other.

### Biot-Savart Law
Empirical Law that defines the magnetic field generated by a steady current.
Expressed as
$$
\vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int \frac{I\times\mathscr{\hat{r}}}{\mathscr{r}^2}\,dl'
$$
where $\mathscr{r} = r-r'$, and $\mu_0=4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ N/A$^2$ and the unit of $\vec{B}$ would be Tesla (T) = N/(Am).

The cross product indicates that the magnetic field is perpendicular to both the direction of the current and the separation vector.

Note that this law cannot be used to calculate the field generated by discrete moving charges (as accelerating charges generates both EM and B fields).

### Curl of a Magnetic Field
A loop path encloses an area, and current is "enclosed" if it has a net flux penetrating through the area. The orientation of this area will depend on the loop path direction.

Line current:
$$
I_\text{enc} = \sum_i I_i
$$
Volume current:
$$
I_\text{enc} = \int \vec{J} \cdot d\vec{a}
$$

### Symmetry of Polar Vectors and Axial Vectors
Given the properties of vectors under electro-magnetostatics, we have to choose which are the polar vectors and which are the axial vectors (also known as [[Pseudovectors]]).

We choose $\vec{B}$ as an axial vector, such that if it is flipped, the sign must also change to account for the reflection of its corresponding $\vec{I}$.
