### Three Pillars of Islamic Art
##### Calligraphy
Why is calligraphy important to Islamic Art?
- The word of God (Allah) is important to Islam
- Possibly aniconism---taboo of depicting human figures, especially in a religious context (see [[#Popular Misconceptions]])
- The word is unchanged, and as such creativity arises in how that unchanged word is written---that is why there are a variety of scripts in Islamic art

Examples of Calligraphy
- Spanish script
	- Kufic script in heading
	- Maghribi script in body text
	- Presence of various medallions -> markers of a new verse
- Iran or Afghanistan Script
	- Hanging script, diagonally as if they are flying birds
	- The style is used for poetry
- Suleiman's Seal
	- Why intricate calligraphy? Harder to copy
		- Alternate interpretation---connection to Allah, as if Allah also approves of
		- Also, to show the seal holder's power and wealth
	- Important at the time when printing did not exist
##### Arabesque (Vegetal Patterns)
- Interlacing leaves, vines, flowers, stems, patterns
- Stylized, abstracted, symmetric
- Flowers in arabesque art versus flower depicted in an encyclopedia/natural sciences book
	- 2d vs 3d
	- infinite vs finite
	- abstract vs scientifically correct/natural
##### Geometry
- Patterned windows (e.g. in Mughal India, sandstone windows)
- Complex layers of patterns of stars, polygon, knots, squares, triangles, quadrilaterals
- Connected to developments in math and algebra
- Wide usage of pen and paper
	- Counterargument: engineering and various complex designs also existed before this, pen and paper just made it easier
#### Combination
- Two possible setups:
	- Calligraphy is main body, geometry and arabesque are fillers/margins
	- Geometry and arabesque is main body, calligraphy is filler/margins
### Popular Misconceptions
- Horror vacui (fear of empty space)
	- Older scholars thought Islamic artists fear empty space because they came from empty or barren lands (deserts)
	- Counterargument: takes away the agency of creativity and rationality present in these artists
- Aniconism
	- Does not actually appear in the Quran, rather in the Hadiths (oral traditions of the deeds of Muhammad)
	- Interpretation: an artist can imitate life, but they must realize that they cannot "breathe life" into their art
		- [[#Countering Orthodoxes|Akbar had this viewpoint]]
		- Only Allah alone has the capacity for life
	- Interpretations vary: many instances in Islamic art of figurative art
		- Mode of consumption of these figurative art is private, respectful
	- Older scholarship: Patrons of figurative imagery were less religious, eccentric
		- UNTRUE
		- Many were champions of their faith
	- Contexts for figurative tradition
		- Does not appear in Quaran (but does appear in poetry, texts laced w/ quaranic verses/quotations)
		- Does not appear on most public or official manifestations of Islam (architecture, coins)
		- Appears in religious architecture: mosques and madrasas
		- Appears often in private world of Muslims: objects of daily use, books of science, history and poetry, textiles, palaces, etc.
	- Why did this misconception form:
		- To avoid polytheism and idolatry
		- Fear that faith may move towards this when figurative religious art existed
### Countering Orthodoxes
##### Example 1: Kamal al din Bihzad
- Consecrated as a true saint (wali): sanctification of a figurative painter
- Comparison of the sacredness of the pen with the brush---poets supported them
##### Example 2: Akbar of the Mughal Empire
- Akbar liberated painting from sectarian ideologies and orthdoxy
- From Abu'l Fazl, his biographer:
	- According to Akbar: the artists would be the first to realize their limits, and so they are closer to the message of the Hadith and they are the ones that respect this fact first and foremost---they are forced to think of God
