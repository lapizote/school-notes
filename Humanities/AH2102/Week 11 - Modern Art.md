### Olympia by Edouard Manet vs Cabanel's Venus
- Shown in the Salon on
- Created a scandal to all involved, even though Birth of Venus was accepted
	- Why?
	- Not because of the women in the nude, because why was Birth of Venus accepted?
		- The feminine nude has always appeared in multiple points in time
		- Perhaps the treatment of the subject, how it's presented?
			- Olympia is a prostitute, Venus is a divine god
				- Olympia dons a confrontational gaze towards the aristocratic audience, vs the demure Venus that averts our gaze
			- Setting: Commodification of the woman's body, unclean setting, versus the romanticized naturalist setting of Venus (or even the upper class setting of Vecellio's Venus)
		- Idealized Venus --- porcelain like flesh, concealed brushstrokes
		- Olympia --- rough brushstrokes, sickly yellow-ish skin
	- Artistic canon is unsettled, tampered with
- Many thought it was incomplete, unfinished
### Les Demoiselles d'Avignon by Picasso
- Distortation, rules of perspective are dismantled, all the conventions of the Renaissance is deconstructed and disintegrated
- Cubist art
- Much like Manet, also looking at lower class women (also sex workers)
### Summary of Readings of the Three Artworks
- Departure from/Rejection of the Past
- Societal Change
- Privileges Newness
- These three are not definitions of modern art, but are frameworks to view and analyze it
- Modern art is not interested in grand themes, aristocracy, or the like---they are more interested in everyday life
### Modern Art
- 1860s to 1950s (in Europe)
- to 1980s (in Africa and Asia)
- We are no longer in this age, as Modern Art was specific to conditions at that time
	- Art now is contemporary art
##### Academy
- Place for artists with similar ideas to come with conversation
- Also a training ground for artists
##### Salon
- Place to exhibit art
- Open to the public
- The board that chooses which art to exhibit are from the Academy
- What were usually exhibited (1640s)?
	- History painting (highest form of academy-style paintings)
	- Royal painting
	- Of grand themes
- After the French Revolution: monarchy lost control of the Salon, attendees of Salon diversifies (of different classes, different backgrounds, nationalities)
	- Art is no longer for the elite
- Artists and attendees were disappointed in the board, as they made consistently conservative and conventional decisions
	- They wanted to see bolder, newer topics
	- Artists broke away from the academy and the salon

### Modernization and Societal Change
- Many changes
	- Industrialization
	- Urbanization
	- New modes of governance
	- Demographic shifts
	- Commodification
	- Alienation
	- Time
- Artists did not simply observe changes and document them, rather they responded, questioned, and critiqued them
### Impressionism
- Pissarro's impressionist painting of Avenue de l'Opera
	- Exhibited change in Paris
	- Widened streets to keep it clean and to keep traffic going
	- Second reason: political decisions, as rebels tended to use narrow alleyways
		- Control
- Impressionism captures impressions/experiences of the new Paris
	- "Fleeting" and ephemeral due to the presence of rough and thick brush strokes
	- The way light moves, people move throughout the day
- Degas: tight focus, inspired by Japanese woodprints
	- As if the frame is "cropped"
	- Of various scenes in Parisian life
	- The average European modern artist was interested in the bold colors and framing of Japanese prints
- In fact, plenty of European modern artists in general had inspiration from foreign ideas (e.g. Picasso was inspired by the African masks/objects in European museums)
### Dadaism
- Other movements were simply to subvert
- Meanwhile, Dadaism was in response to the first World War and bourgeois culture
	- To critique and reject modernization
- Varied across different regions
- Marcel Duchamp's Fountain:
	- Who defines art?
- Criticizes the art ecosystem at the time
- Experimental forms and multimedia
