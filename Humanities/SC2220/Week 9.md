### Gender and Fear
- Fear is a cultural product
- Reflective of asymmetrical gendered power relations operating within social interactions and spaces
- Experienced by individuals who occupy relatively marginal and powerless positions in society
- Women experience more anxieties in public spaces as they are perceived to be the "weaker sex" in regards to attackers
- Sexual harassment is seen as exclusively a "womens' issue"

Earlier research:
- Women's fears: Sexual harassment and assault by male
- Men's fears: violent and physical crimes; being mistaken as perpetrators

##### Intersectionality
- Women's identities are not a monolith:
	- Gendered fear of sexual harassment intersects with other social identities (e.g. homophobia, transpobia, racism, islamophobia, etc.)
- Social spaces are not a monolith: Fear is also dependent on physical and social characteristics of the particular space
- Situating agency (i.e. women's responses to their fears) within the society's construction of heteropatriarchy
- Age: Older women may see themselves and socially vulnerable
- Class: Female expatriates may feel safer compared to their home countries, as well as staying in gated communities
- Gendered fear of sexualized crime by women is socialized by:
	- Parents and peers
	- Personal experiences
	- Media
	- Built environment (e.g. dark and secluded areas, crime prevention posters)
### Example 1: Crime Prevention Posters on Public Transport and Transits
- Public transport is a hotspot of sexualized crime
- Crime-prevention posters often depict feminine-prsenting victim vs. masculine presenting perpetrator
- Contradicts the portrayal of media that Singapore is safe even for women
- Earlier iterations were criticized for having victim-blaming undertones
	- e.g. emphasis on ways women can protect themselves
- Echoes a very individualistic "solution" to sexual offences where potential victims are responsible for their safety
- Later iterations emphasize legal consequences and harsh punishments rather than addressing structural issues and support for victims
- Mainstream media reports often ignore masculinity of perpetrator
	- Failure to consider structural factors behind sexual offences (i.e. heteropatriarchy)
	- Not a "genderless" crime: occluding masculinity while still existing in a patriarchy is not just hegemonic, but also invisible

### Gender Performativity and (Landscapes of) Fear
- The Paradox of Masculinity
	- Males are framed as dangerous, but also framed as protectors
	- Masculine spaces are seen as risky for women
- As women gradually learn that men control public streets and being unaccompanied is "fair game for sexual attack"
- Reflected in indivdual strategies:
	- Avoidance of certain place, at a certain time
	- Pretending to be on a phone call
	- Informing male perpetrators that they are meeting w/ boyfriend
	- Being chaperoned, sometimes by a male figure
- Under heteropatriarchy, femininity is seen as subordinate and vulnerable
- Gender is not just self-identity, it is a repeated and embodied [[Week 4 - Constructions of Gender and Sexuality I|performatively]]
- Female-only spaces are introduced as protective measure for women against sexual harassment
	- Form of gendered spatial governance on who belongs, based on gender and gender expression
	- Individuals are expected to pass as (cis-)women
	- Androgynous/masculine-presenting women express anxiety with using female toilets due to experiences with genderism
		- Genderism marginalizes trans people
		- e.g. Trans individuals are sometimes subjected to extra scrutiny like card checks
- Constructions of hegemonic gender relations under heteropatriarchy -> women are perceived to be socially and sexually weaker
- Erasure of other female subjectivities for those who transgress binary gender norms
### The "Respectable" Femininity
- Pressure to modify gender expression such as physical appearances to "pass" as heterosexual
- Experiences of internalized homophobia regardless of actual sexuality
- Red-light districts are spaces where women's everyday experiences of harassment and trivialized and normalized
- Forms of "immoral" heterosexuality are encapsulated through sex workers and the performance of hypersexuality
	- Paradoxically, women are afraid of being thought of as a sex worker AND encountering a sex worker
- Women, particularly sex workers, are "constantly gazed at and harassed" by men
	- Embodying "too much" femininity would mean drawing unwanted attention
- These spaces continually occupy a marginal space in Singapore
	- They challenge and disrupt the narrow definition of hegemonic heterosexual discourse revolvig around "chastity" of femininity
	- Echoes of needing to be chaste, conservative, and not too hyper-feminine and hyper-sexualized were extended to everyday socialization and decision-making
- Strategies to 'desexualize' from the male gaze---looking bulkier and androgynous-looking
- "Respectable" femininity is a balancing act
- Expectations of women to be heterosexual and reproductive, yet embodying chaste and "right amount" of femininity
### Conclusions:
- Fear of older and deviant male strangers: Men and hegemonic masculinity are perceived as risky AND as protectors
- Homophobia, transphobia, and genderism: transgressive bodily expressions and behaviors are "read" as queer; expectations that one should conform to their biological sex
- Anxieties around 'immoral' and 'scary' femininities: Hyper-femininity and hypersexuality goes against Asian "respectability"
- Situated agency: Responses to fears and anxieties reflects hegemonic social structures and are rooted in heteropatriarchy
	- Spatial and temporal avoidance of 'risky' masculine spaces
	- Changes in gender expressions
