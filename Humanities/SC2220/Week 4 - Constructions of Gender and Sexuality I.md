Where does gender come from, so to speak?
### Culture
"Human nature is almost unbelievably malleable (...) the personality traits which we have called masculine or feminine are as lightly linked to sex as are the clothing, the manners, and the form of head-dress that a society at a given period assigns to either sex." - Margaret Mead, *Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies* (1935)
- From 1931-1933 Mead did fieldwork in northern Papua New Guinea
- Three different ethnic groups
- Interested in attributes associated with women and men
##### Arapesh
- similar characteristics with women and men
- unaggressive
- cooperative
- responsive to needs of others
- nurturing
##### Mundugumor/Biwat
- similar characteristics with women and men
- aggressive and violent
- domineering
- focuses on status
##### Tchambuli/Chambri
- had marked gender differences
- Women:
	- impersonal
	- managerial
	- dominant partner
- Men:
	- irresponsibe
	- emotionally dependent
##### Outcome
- Mead argued that differences between sexes must be learned
- Throughout life (especially in childhood), people are conditioned to behave to conform to norms
### Childhood
- "boying the boy" and "girling the girl" starts before birth
- "Gender" reveal parties
- Colors and designs on baby products
- Children are disciplined into appropriate forms of gendered behavior by family
- Institutions, most notably schools, teach "gender lessons"
- Peers reinforce gender norms
	- Not just adults, even children teach each other
- Companies sell gendered children's products
	- e.g. in the late 1980s, Disney created a market for princess products for girls
	- Reproduces dominant constructions of gender based on type of activity, colors, advertisements, etc.
- In some contexts: permissible (or celebrated) for girls to "act like boys"---tomboys
	- Rare for the other way around
	- Male characteristics are more highly valued
	- No longer as permissible starting adolescence
### Performativity
- Judith Butler - PhD in Philosophy
- *Gender Trouble: Feminism and the Subversion of Identity* (1990)
	- "There is no gender identity behind the expressions of gender; that identity is performatively constituted by the very 'expressions' that are said to be its results."
	- Gender isn't something we are, it's something we do
- Gender is an 'act', broadly construed, which constructs the social fiction of its own psychological interiority"
	- People (unconsciously) behave in ways associated with femininity or masculinity
	- Repetition of these acts makes it seem as if gender is a natural expression of who we are
	- To be performative, something needs to produce effects
- Not that people are constrained by norms
	- "Perhaps we should think of gender as something imposed at birth, through sex assignment and all the culture assumptions that usually go along with that. Yet gender is also what is made along the way - we can take over the power of assignment, make it into self-assignment"
	- "Gender then becomes a negotiation, a struggle, a way of dealing with historical constraints and making new realities" - Gender can be liberatory
- Gender is also "constituted through the force of exclusion and abjection, one which produces a constitutive outside to the subject, an abjected outside"
	- People (unconsciously) affirm their identities as "normal" and culturally intelligible by repudiating unacceptably gendered selves
	- "Normal" gender identities often assumed to be heterosexual
### Heteronormativity
- Ways that heterosexuality is granted as the only "normal" or "natural" form of sexuality
- Disparages and/or erases other forms of sexuality
- Privileges particular kinds of heterosexual relationships
- Part of patriarchy, in that it makes women dependent on men
### What is masculinity?
- In the 1990s, increasing scholarly interest in men and masculinity
- Shift initiated by feminists from treating "people" as synonymous with "men"
- Masculinity sometimes thought of as anything men think or do
	- Or anything men think or do to be men
- Looking at variety of masculinities across and within particular cultural contexts
	- R.W. Connell: identified types of masculinity
	- Hegemonic masculinity
		- type of gender practice that supports gender inequality and marginalizes other ways of being a man
		- Toxic masculinity
- Recent scholarship sees it as configuration of practices and discourses
	- Different people can embody masculinity in different ways, to different degrees
	- Not linked to bodies assigned male at birth
	- Same can be said for femininity
	- e.g. C.J. Pascoe on American youth
- Masculinity as "an identity expressed through sexual discourses and practice that indicated dominance and control"
	- Boys claimed masculine identities via homophobic comments and by asserting control over girls' bodies
	- (Cisgender) girls sometimes called masculine if they were dominant
