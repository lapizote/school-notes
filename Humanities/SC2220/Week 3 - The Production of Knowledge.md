### Feminist Anthropology
- Broad term that encompasses work on gender
	- Attentiveness to intersecton of power and oppression
	- Awareness of how knowledge is producted
- US Context: Emerges early 1970s
	- Alongside women's liberation movement
- Marginalized within academy
### Androcentrism
- Anthropology was largely produced by men, about men
	- Androcentric - male-centric
- Few prominent women in discipline's early history - exceptions that proved the rule
- Ethnographies rarely included info about women's activities
	- Their perspectives were missing
	- Portrayed as if they passively cooperated with men's desires
### "Discovery" of Women
- Feminist anthropologists focused on women's lives and perspectives
- Ethnographies revealed different insights than those produced by/about men would have
	- E.g. Margery Wolf - *Women and the Family in Rural Taiwan*
- Returned to previous research sites
	- Explore what male ethnographers missed
	- E.g.
		- Jane Goodale - *Tiwi Wives*
			- Annette Weiner - *Women of Value, Men of Renown: New Perspectives in Trobriand Exchange*
- Analyzed women's experience of oppression in many contexts
- Analyzed societies with greater egalitarianism
- Work as both intellectual and political project
	- Intellectual knowledge allows us to change aspects of society
	- Commitment to social justice remains today
	- All knowledge is political
### Heterogeneity
- 1980s: heightened awareness that 'women' cannot be treated as a homogenous category
- Women of color drew attention to intersectionality
- Dynamics of colonization:
	- "a relation of domination, and a discursive or political suppression of the heterogeneity of the subject(s) in question"
##### Mohanty's Article
- Analyzes how western feminist scholarship colonized the lives of women in Global South
	- Creates an image of a singular 'third world woman'
- Sexually constrained, ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, religious, etc.
- Contrast to implicit self-representation of the writers
- Frames women in Global South as 'Others' and 'objects' without agency - perpetuates the 'hegemony of the idea of the superiority of the west' (p. 81)
	- Assumption that women in the Global South are ignorant of their reality
### From Women to Gender
- 1990s: Gender becomes focus of analysis, rather than women
- Work illustrates how the 'woman' category is a cultural construct that varies significantly
- Shifts attention to heterogeneity
- Makes room for the study of men and other genders
### Queer Anthropology
- 1970s: Little research on sexuality
- 1980s: Somewhat autonomous field of lesbian and gay anthropology emerges
	- E.g. Ellen Lewin - *Lesbian Mothers: Accounts of Gender in American Culture*
- 1990s: Shift to term *queer*
	- Signals that which is non-normative and transgressive - Margot Weiss
	- Used to flag 'an insistence on potentiality or concrete possibility for another world' - Jafari Allen
### Situated Knowledges
- Donna Haraway: feminist scholar in science and technology studies
- 1988
- Part of a larger conversation by feminists across disciplines about how knowledge is produced
### Knowledge
- People create knowledge - ask questions and interpret data
- Knowledge is shaped by perspective of individual
	- Shaped by gender, race, class, etc.
- E.g. Malinowski vs Weiner on Trobriander exchanges
	- Main difference: gender
	- Not only androcentrism, but gender norms around segregation of genders may have made it difficult for Malinowski to look into women's perspectives
- Also shaped by particular historical circumstances under which it was made
- Consider: broader political economic situation
	- What kind of research receives funding?
	- What kind of questions are considered valuable?
	- Whose concerns are treated as important?
	- E.g. Research on men's health vs women's health in the UK
### "God Trick"
- Knowledge often appears as if it wasn't made by articular people under particular historical circumstances
- "...conquering gaze from nowhere. This is the gaze that mythically inscribes all the marked bodies, that makes the unmarked category claim the power to see and not be seen, to represent while escaping representation. This gaze signifies the unmarked positions of White and Man."
- Knowledge is presented from a disembodied perspective
	- Treated as 'objective' or 'neutral' mostly produced by white men
	- Knowledge produced by women and POC are seen as 'niche' and less valued
- Knowledge is power - shapes practices and policies which benefit some over others; this matters!
### Alternatives
- Very strong social constructivist stance: there is no 'reality' out there to be discovered, in the sense that all knowledge is produced
- Haraway wants more than this
- Calls for 'simultaneously an account of radical historical contingency for all knowledge claims... and a no-nonsense commitment to faithful accounts of a "real" world' (p.579)
### Situated Knowledges
- Recognizes that all knowledge is situated (i.e. coming from a distinct perspective)
	- This is not to be hidden
- 'Objectivity turns out to be about particular and specific embodiment and definitely not about the false vision promising transcendence of all limits and responsibility. The moral is simple: only partial perspective promises objective vision.' (p. 582)
- Make connections between partial perspectives
	- From there, we can see an objective view of the real world
- "Many currents in feminism attempt to theorize grounds for trusting "
	- We need other perspectives, particularly those who have been marginalized
	- Those with less power have knowledge of their own experiences as well as some sense of those of people in power - reverse case is rare
### Reflexivity
- Acknowledging how your positionality influences all aspects of the research process
	- Questions you ask
	- Interactions with other people
	- Interpretation of data
- Positionality: your social position with a particular context based on aspects of your identity (i.e. how much power you have based on gender, age, nationality, etc.)
- Do not pretend that you are neutral
- Developed by feminists
- Becomes standard anthropological convention (to varying degrees)
	- Texts will include discussion of who the researcher is
	- Researcher's voice incorporated throughout (written in first-person)
